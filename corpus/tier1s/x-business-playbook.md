# Advertising on X: A Playbook from X's Official Advertiser Guidance

*Original synthesis of knowledge learned from X Business advertiser guidance. Ideas are expressed in Marketr's own words; factual attribution to X's official curriculum/guidance.*

## 1. Start with the objective model

X organizes campaigns around three layers of intent, and picking the right one is the single most important decision you will make:

- **Awareness** — your goal is reach. You want as many of the right people as possible to see the message. The system optimizes for impressions and visibility.
- **Consideration** — you want an action that signals interest: video views, clicks through to a website, engagement (likes, replies, reposts), or app installs.
- **Conversion** — you want a bottom-funnel outcome: a purchase or signup on your website, or getting existing app users to come back and take action (app re-engagement).

The discipline here is choosing **one objective per campaign**. Mixing objectives inside a campaign confuses delivery — the optimization system cannot decide what success looks like. If you need both awareness and conversions, run two campaigns with two distinct objectives, not one blended campaign.

## 2. Keep the structure simple while you learn

The official guidance is blunt about early account structure:

1. **Begin with one campaign, one objective, one ad group.** This concentrates delivery and gives the learning system clean signal.
2. **Then split ad groups by audience, not by creative.** Want to test men 25–34 in Texas versus marketing-interested users nationally? Two ad groups, identical creative. Want to test creative? Keep the audience constant. One variable per test or you will never know what moved the number.
3. **Only add ad groups when you can fund them.** Every ad group needs enough budget to exit learning. Dozens of micro ad groups with tiny budgets starve each other.

As a rule of thumb: consolidate until performance is stable, then expand. Fragmentation early is the most common beginner mistake on the platform.

## 3. Let the system bid for you at first

Automatic bidding exists for a reason — X's delivery system has far more auction data than you do. The recommended progression:

- Start on **automatic bidding** while the account learns what your conversions and engagements are worth.
- Watch cost-per-result for a full week (two is better) before touching anything.
- Only move to bid caps or target costs when you have a statistically meaningful volume of conversions and a number you genuinely need to hold.

Manual bidding too early constrains delivery before the system knows who converts for you. Patience in week one buys efficiency in month two.

## 4. Build audiences from signals, not guesses

X offers several audience dimensions; the art is combining them in layers:

- **Demographics** — age, gender, language. Keep this broad unless your product genuinely skews.
- **Location** — country, region, city. Essential for local businesses; otherwise think about where your buyers actually live.
- **Device** — target iOS, Android, or desktop when the product experience differs by device (an iOS app install campaign should not buy Android impressions).
- **Interests and behaviors** — the platform infers what people follow, engage with, and talk about. Interest targeting is the workhorse for prospecting.
- **Follower lookalikes** — patterns modeled on the followers of specific accounts (yours, or accounts whose audiences resemble your buyers). This is X's closest equivalent to lookalike audiences and tends to be high quality when seeded from the right accounts.

A practical stacking order: start with a broad interest-based audience, layer on follower-lookalike patterns for precision, and reserve narrow demographic slices for later refinement. Always keep one broad "discovery" audience running so you do not over-narrow yourself out of scale.

## 5. Creative must speak the feed's native language

This is where most advertisers fail on X. The feed is a discourse environment — opinions, data, takes, arguments, threads. A polished thirty-second brand spot dropped into that context reads as an interruption and gets scrolled past.

What works instead:

- **Write like a person, not a brand.** Short copy with a point of view outperforms generic slogans. Lead with the take, the stat, or the contrarian claim.
- **Use threads as creative.** A well-structured thread is both ad unit and content marketing — it earns the tap because it promises substance.
- **Show data and proof.** Screenshots, charts, before/after numbers — the feed rewards receipts.
- **Match the platform's cadence.** Posts should feel like they belong in a timeline, not in a media plan. UGC-style video and lo-fi imagery typically beat studio production here.

## 6. Use polls and replies as engagement mechanics

X has native interaction formats that other platforms lack, and they double as cheap research:

- **Polls** are engagement magnets — voting is nearly frictionless, and each vote is a signal you can retarget or analyze. Run opinion polls about your category; the results are content in themselves.
- **Replies as CTA.** Instead of "click the link," try "reply with your biggest challenge and we'll answer." Conversations boost organic reach and surface objection language you can reuse in copy.
- **Quote-post prompts** — asking people to share their take with a prompt produces social proof at scale.

Treat every engagement as a data point: what people vote for, argue about, and ask for tells you what creative to make next.

## 7. Frequency discipline

Because X is a high-velocity feed, your ad can appear in front of the same user many times. Set frequency expectations deliberately:

- Monitor average frequency per user alongside reach — high frequency with flat engagement means you are paying to annoy people.
- Refresh creative before frequency climbs into the danger zone; on X the refresh cycle is faster than on most platforms.
- Use frequency as a diagnostic: rising frequency plus declining click-through usually means the audience is exhausted and it is time to broaden or swap creative.

## 8. Treat benchmarks as starting points, not targets

Cost benchmarks on X vary dramatically by industry, geography, and objective. Platform-published averages are useful for one thing: knowing roughly what neighborhood you are in during week one. They are not performance targets.

The correct approach:

- Record your own baseline costs per result in the first two weeks.
- Judge optimization against your own trendline, not an industry table.
- Break out costs by audience and creative so you know which combination is pulling the average up or down.

## 9. Measure with proper tagging

None of the above works without measurement infrastructure:

- **Install X's website/conversion tag** on every page that matters — not just the purchase confirmation, but the steps leading to it (view, add-to-cart, start checkout). Funnel visibility is what lets you diagnose where creative versus landing page is failing.
- For app campaigns, integrate the mobile measurement partner so install and re-engagement events flow back into the ad account.
- Give the tag a full learning window before judging performance — conversion data arrives with delay, and optimizing on day-two numbers leads to bad decisions.

## 10. The launch checklist

1. Tag installed and firing on all key events (verify with a test conversion).
2. One campaign per objective; start with one ad group.
3. Automatic bidding selected; target cost left unset until baseline established.
4. Two to three audiences built: one broad interest, one follower-lookalike, one test variant.
5. Five or more creative variants written in native feed voice — takes, threads, proof, polls.
6. Frequency monitoring in place; creative refresh scheduled.
7. Two-week measurement window committed before any structural changes.
