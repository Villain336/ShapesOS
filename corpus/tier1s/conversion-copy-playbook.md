# Conversion Copy Playbook

*Original synthesis of knowledge learned from the Copyhackers blog's tutorials. Marketr-original words; factual attribution to Copyhackers (Joanna Wiebe).*

## Copy is salesmanship in print — act like it

Every page has a job, and the copy's job is to move the visitor one step closer to the action. Before writing a word, answer three questions: **who is this for, what do they want, and what must they believe to act?** If you can't answer all three, you're not ready to write — you're ready to research.

## Research: the 80% of copywriting nobody sees

Great copy is assembled, not invented. The research stack:

1. **Voice-of-customer mining.** Reviews (yours and competitors'), support tickets, sales call transcripts, survey responses, forum threads. Collect the exact phrases customers use — their words, not your marketing words. A headline in the customer's language outperforms a clever headline every time.
2. **The four buyer questions.** For any offer, buyers need answers to: *What is it?* (clear description), *What's in it for me?* (outcome, not features), *Why should I believe you?* (proof), and *Why now?* (urgency). Map every section of the page to one of these questions; unmapped sections get cut.
3. **Competitor teardown.** Document how competitors describe the same offer, what proof they use, and where their messaging is vague or arrogant. Your differentiation lives in the gaps they leave.
4. **Objection inventory.** List every reason a prospect might say no — price, switching cost, "will it work for me," trust, timing. Each objection gets a dedicated answer somewhere on the page. Unanswered objections don't disappear; they just kill the sale silently.

## Page architecture: the conversion spine

1. **Headline — the 80/20 of the page.** It must communicate the offer's core value in the visitor's language, ideally with specificity (numbers, timeframes, outcomes). Clarity beats cleverness: a confused visitor bounces; a bored-but-clear visitor reads on.
2. **Subheadline — the bridge.** Expands the headline: who it's for, the mechanism, or the differentiator. If the headline is the promise, the subheadline is the first proof.
3. **The sticky bar / hero CTA** — one action, repeated. The CTA button should complete the sentence "I want to ___" (get the template, start my trial, see pricing). Never "Submit" — submit is what you do to authority.
4. **Problem/agitation** — show you understand their pain in their words. The prospect should think "that's exactly my situation" before you present a single feature.
5. **The mechanism** — how it works, in 3 steps or fewer. People buy mechanisms they understand; "magic AI" is not a mechanism.
6. **Proof stacking** — testimonials, results, logos, data, guarantees — layered near each claim, not quarantined on a separate page. Every bold claim should have proof within scrolling distance.
7. **Feature → benefit translation** — every feature gets a "which means" clause. "Automated workflows" means "no more manual data entry on Fridays." Features tell, benefits sell, and the translation is the copywriter's actual job.
8. **Risk reversal** — guarantees, free trials, "cancel anytime." The stronger the guarantee, the weaker the prospect's last objection. A guarantee you can afford to offer is a guarantee worth featuring.
9. **Close and FAQ** — restate the offer plainly, then answer the objection inventory directly. FAQs aren't filler; they're the highest-converting section on many pages because they answer the questions asked at the moment of decision.

## Voice and style rules

- **Write like one human talking to one human.** Second person, active voice, short sentences. Read every draft aloud — if you stumble, the reader stumbles.
- **Specificity is persuasion.** "Helps teams work better" is noise. "Cuts weekly reporting from 4 hours to 20 minutes" is copy. Numbers, names, timeframes, and concrete nouns do the convincing; adjectives do the decorating.
- **Cut mercilessly.** Every sentence must earn its place: does it answer a buyer question, handle an objection, or advance the action? If not, delete it. Long copy that sells beats short copy that doesn't — but only if every paragraph works.
- **Match the awareness stage.** Problem-aware visitors need education and empathy; solution-aware visitors need comparison and differentiation; product-aware visitors need proof and the offer. Writing solution-aware copy to a problem-aware audience is the most common messaging mismatch.

## Email and microcopy

- **Subject lines:** curiosity or clarity, never clickbait. The subject line's only job is the open — but a misleading subject that gets opened and deleted trains the spam filter and the subscriber simultaneously.
- **Buttons and forms:** microcopy at the action point has outsized leverage. "Get my free template" vs. "Download" can swing conversion double digits. Error messages should blame the system, never the user ("that email looks incomplete — mind checking it?").
- **Testimonials that sell:** the best testimonials mirror the prospect's objection and resolve it ("I was worried it wouldn't integrate with our stack, but setup took 20 minutes"). Curate for objection-handling, not praise.

## The editing pass

After drafting: cut 20% (at least), replace every adjective with a fact, check every claim for proof, read aloud once more, and hand it to someone who knows nothing about the product — if they can't explain the offer back to you, the page failed. Then test: headlines, CTAs, and offer framing are the highest-leverage test targets.
