# Startup Acquisition Playbook

*Original synthesis of knowledge learned from the Demand Curve blog's playbook-style articles. Marketr-original words; factual attribution to Demand Curve.*

## Find your one channel before you diversify

Early-stage startups don't have a marketing mix — they have a search for **one channel that works**, then a doubling-down. The doctrine: test channels cheaply and in parallel, identify the one with the best economics at small scale, then pour fuel on it until it saturates. Spreading a small budget across six channels produces six inconclusive experiments.

## The channel menu (test in this order for most B2B/B2C startups)

1. **Content + SEO** — slowest to start, highest compounding. Works when buyers research before buying (B2B SaaS, considered purchases). Start with bottom-of-funnel content (comparisons, alternatives, use-case pages) before top-of-funnel — capture existing demand before creating it.
2. **Paid search** — fastest signal. If people search for your category, paid search tells you within weeks whether your positioning converts. Start with exact/phrase match on high-intent terms; it's expensive but it's truth.
3. **Paid social** — best for products with visual/demo appeal or novel categories people don't search for. Creative is the whole game; budget for creative production like it's media spend.
4. **Email/lifecycle** — not an acquisition channel per se, but the highest-ROI channel for converting acquired attention into revenue. Build the list from day one; every visitor who leaves without subscribing is acquisition spend wasted.
5. **Partnerships and integrations** — co-marketing with complementary products, integration marketplaces, affiliate programs. Slow to set up, durable once running.
6. **Community and word-of-mouth** — founder-led content, niche communities, referral loops. Unscalable in the best way: the insights compound even when the channel doesn't.
7. **Outbound (B2B)** — cold email and LinkedIn outreach to tightly defined ICPs. Works when ACV justifies the effort ($5k+). Personalization at the account level, not the merge-tag level.

## Landing pages that convert cold traffic

- **Message match:** the headline echoes the ad or search query that brought the visitor. A click on "project management for agencies" that lands on generic "work better together" copy bounces.
- **One page, one action.** Cold traffic gets one CTA. Navigation, footer links, and secondary offers are exit ramps — remove them.
- **Above the fold:** headline (outcome in the visitor's words), subheadline (mechanism or differentiator), visual (product or outcome), CTA, and one trust signal. Everything else is supporting cast.
- **Social proof early and specific.** Logos, numbers ("4,200 agencies"), and short testimonial quotes above the fold. Cold visitors decide in seconds whether you're legitimate.

## The growth math: CAC, LTV, and payback

- **Unit economics before scale.** Know your customer acquisition cost by channel, lifetime value (or 12-month revenue for early startups), and the LTV:CAC ratio. Below 3:1, fix the business before scaling spend; above 3:1 with fast payback, the constraint is usually courage, not capital.
- **Payback period is the real constraint.** A channel with great LTV:CAC but 18-month payback will starve a startup of cash. Optimize for payback first, ratio second.
- **Cohort analysis, not averages.** Blended metrics hide everything. Cut by channel, by month, by plan tier. The channel that's "too expensive" on average may have a cohort that's wildly profitable.

## Retention is acquisition (the leaky bucket)

Acquiring users who churn in month one is not growth — it's a treadmill. Before scaling acquisition:

1. **Measure retention curves** by cohort. If the curve doesn't flatten, you have a product problem, not a marketing problem, and no channel will save you.
2. **Onboard to the aha moment.** Identify the action that correlates with retention (the "aha moment") and design onboarding to get every new user there fast. Email sequences, in-app checklists, and concierge onboarding for high-value accounts.
3. **Win back the churned.** Exit surveys, win-back sequences, and "here's what's new" campaigns to lapsed users are the cheapest acquisition channel you already own.

## Pricing and packaging as a growth lever

- **Packaging is positioning.** Good-better-best tiers let customers self-select; the middle tier should be the obvious choice (the decoy effect is real, use it honestly).
- **Annual plans** improve cash flow and retention — discount them meaningfully (2 months free is the standard anchor) and push them at the point of highest enthusiasm (right after signup, after first win).
- **Free trials vs. freemium:** trials create urgency and qualify intent; freemium creates a user base and word-of-mouth but monetizes slowly. Match the model to the product's time-to-value: fast value → trial; slow/network-effect value → freemium.

## The operating cadence

Weekly: review channel metrics, ship one experiment per active channel, fix the biggest funnel leak. Monthly: cohort review, budget reallocation toward winners, kill the bottom channel. Quarterly: step back — is the one channel still the one channel, or is it saturating? Saturation shows up as rising CAC at constant volume; the answer is the next channel, found the same way as the first: cheap parallel tests.
